# Learning from space games: two studies

Summaries of two studies the team brought in on 15 Nov 2026, and how they map onto LEAP's two modes (Explorer and
Mission Director, ticket 53). Used in the project description (`docs/submission/leap-project-description.tex`).
Metadata checked against Crossref.

## Cao, Peng and Hansberger (2019): a VR game of an Apollo mission

L. Cao, C. Peng, J. T. Hansberger, "Usability and Engagement Study for a Serious Virtual Reality Game of Lunar
Exploration Missions", *Informatics* 6(4), 44, 2019. <https://doi.org/10.3390/informatics6040044>

- A game built on the Apollo 16 lunar mission: plan a rover route on a lunar map, pick and load equipment, then drive
  while watching direction, speed and motor or battery temperature.
- 30 participants compared a virtual-reality version with a standard-screen one. VR raised reported interest in space
  science and lunar history, but was harder to operate and took longer; practice closed part of the gap.
- It measured usability, engagement and interest, not factual learning.
- Takeaways: build learning into connected mission phases; give meaningful scientific choices; use authentic mission
  details; balance immersion with ease of use, with practice first; design for accessibility and comfort.

## Herder and Rau (2022): using the tools is not understanding the science

T. Herder, M. A. Rau, "The role of representational competencies for students' learning from an educational video
game for astronomy", *Frontiers in Education* 7, 919645, 2022. <https://doi.org/10.3389/feduc.2022.919645>

- Students played *At Play in the Cosmos*, using tools such as spectrum analysers and radial-velocity displays.
- A case study: a high-performing student operated the tools without clearly understanding the ideas behind them.
- An experiment (45 students, 43 analysed) tested extra prompts. Players got better at recognising patterns in the
  visuals, but showed no significant gain in explaining them or in astronomy knowledge; the prompts did not help
  significantly.
- Takeaways: make interpretation part of progress; connect data to what it shows; keep interfaces uncrowded; follow
  pattern-matching with "why"; check understanding, not just task completion.

## How LEAP uses them

| Finding | Explorer | Mission Director |
|---|---|---|
| Connected mission phases | Contract, design, four-step launch plan, flight, report | Same chain, all seven planning steps |
| Ease of use first, practice before challenge | Short texts, bigger controls, gentler failure, one refund per Contract | Full detail; an Explorer Company can switch up and keep its progress |
| Authentic details | Real parts, rockets, sites, weather, in words and pictures | Real numbers and units, cited facts, margins |
| Connect data to meaning | Ghost preview of each Part's effect on the bars | Six live bars with exact values |
| Interface use is not understanding | Report says in plain words why it worked or failed | Report explains with the design's own numbers; Design Review asks for margins |
| Check understanding | Not measured yet: classroom tests should ask players to explain a failure, not only fix it | Same |
