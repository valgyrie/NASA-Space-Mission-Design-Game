**Game notes**
- Explorer 1 had **batteries only**, so the tutorial Spacecraft uses batteries, not solar. Solar cells unlock after Vanguard 1's real first (1958).
- Explorer 1 Contract: client **US Army**. The Army Ballistic Missile Agency (ABMA) modified the Jupiter-C, and "ABMA and JPL completed the job of modifying the Jupiter-C and building Explorer 1 in 84 days"; Project Orbiter was "a joint U.S. Army and U.S. Navy proposal". Its main instrument was a Geiger tube "to detect cosmic rays". The high-power transmitter ran 31 days, so the game asks for **30 days of Data**.[^e1]
- Telstar shows the Rideshare idea: a private company paying for a government launch.
- Artemis IV is a future event: keep its data in one file and re-check before release.

**Explorer 1 Real History cards (ticket 13, checked 2026-11-10)**
- Before: Sputnik launched **4 Oct 1957**; Vanguard TV3 lost thrust and exploded on the pad **6 Dec 1957**; ABMA and JPL modified the Jupiter-C and built Explorer 1 **in 84 days**; it carried a cosmic-ray detector and was powered by batteries.[^e1card][^e1ten]
- After: the cosmic-ray detector read **far lower** than expected; Van Allen proposed it was **saturated** by charged particles trapped by Earth's magnetic field: the radiation belts later named after him, the first US scientific discovery in space. Data until the batteries died in **May 1958** (one NASA page says last transmission 21 May, another batteries died 23 May, so the card says "May"). NASA began operating **1 Oct 1958**. Re-entered 31 Mar 1970.[^e1card][^e1ten]
- Photos (NASA Image and Video Library, public domain NASA media, credited "NASA/MSFC"): [0100074](https://images.nasa.gov/details/0100074) Explorer 1's launch, 31 Jan 1958; [5663627](https://images.nasa.gov/details/5663627) Pickering, Van Allen and von Braun holding up the full-size Explorer 1 model at the 1 a.m. press conference after launch (described on [NASA Science: Explorer 1](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/explorer-1/)). Resized copies in `public/history/`.

**Vanguard TV3 failure story card (ticket 17, checked 2026-11-10)**

- 6 Dec 1957, Cape Canaveral: about 2 seconds after liftoff, having risen about **1.2 m (4 ft)**, the rocket lost thrust, fell back and exploded, badly damaging the pad. The 1.5 kg satellite was thrown clear with its transmitters still sending; it is now at the National Air and Space Museum. Cause: low fuel tank pressure let hot gas back into the injector; the engine was modified (higher tank pressure, later an ethane start) and "did not fail again". Vanguard 1 reached orbit on 17 Mar 1958.[^vtv3]
- NASA's own history page calls it "the spectacular and embarrassing launch failure of Vanguard TV3 on December 6"; it is the card's **Learn more →**.[^e1card]
- Photo: NASA Image and Video Library [KSC-58PD-06149A](https://images.nasa.gov/details/58PD-06149A) ("Waiting for the Vanguard 1 launch", March 1958, NASA): a Vanguard rocket on its pad. No NASA photo of the TV3 explosion was found in the library; Wikipedia's could not be fetched (rate limited). Resized copy: `public/history/vanguard-rocket.jpg`.

**TIROS-1 and Vanguard 1 Real History cards (ticket 14, checked 2026-11-10)**
- TIROS-1: launched **1 Apr 1960**, 11:40 UTC, Cape Canaveral LC-17A, on a **Thor-Able** (Thor DM-18 Able-II). Built by **RCA** for **NASA**. **122.5 kg** (NASA: 270 lb). 18-sided prism, 107 cm across, 56 cm high. Orbit **693 × 750 km, 48.4°**, 99.16 min. About **9,000** solar cells (NASA says 9,200) charging nickel-cadmium batteries. **Two TV cameras** (wide- and narrow-angle); tape recorder(s) (Wikipedia: one; NASA: one per camera). Spin-stabilised at 8–12 rpm. NASA: "operational for only **78 days**"; Wikipedia: worked until **15 June 1960**, when "an electrical power failure prevented further useful TV transmission" (75 days). Returned about **23,000** pictures, **19,000** usable for weather analysis. Its first wide-angle TV image was taken on 1 April 1960.[^tiros][^tirosnasa]
- The game's TIROS-1 Contract asks for **78 days** of Data (NASA's figure).
- Vanguard 1: launched **17 Mar 1958**; **1.46 kg**, a 152 mm sphere; "the world's first solar-powered satellite"; second US satellite; "remains the oldest artificial object orbiting Earth". Six solar cells ran a 5 mW transmitter until **May 1964**; the battery transmitter stopped in 1958 (NSSDC: June 1958; Wikipedia: tracking signals for 20 days). Began at the Naval Research Laboratory.[^v1][^v1nasa]
- Photos: [NASA image article "Vanguard Satellite, 1958"](https://www.nasa.gov/image-article/vanguard-satellite-1958/) ("One of the Vanguard satellites is checked out at Cape Canaveral, Florida in 1958", credit NASA); NASA Image and Video Library [9141922](https://images.nasa.gov/details/9141922) (Thor/Able 3 launching Explorer 6, August 1959, NASA/MSFC) for the TIROS-1 "before" card, since no TIROS-1 photo was found in the NASA library; [GSFC_20171208_Archive_e000942](https://images.nasa.gov/details/GSFC_20171208_Archive_e000942) (Hurricane Gonzalo from NASA's Terra satellite, 16 Oct 2014, NASA Goddard MODIS Rapid Response) for the "after" card. Resized copies in `public/history/`. Wikimedia's TIROS-1 first-picture file could not be downloaded (rate limited); swap it in later if wanted.

**Telstar 1 and Syncom 3 (ticket 18, checked 2026-11-11)**
- Telstar 1: launched **10 July 1962** at 08:35 UTC from Cape Canaveral **LC-17B** on a **Thor-Delta** (Thor/Delta 316); built by **Bell Labs** for **AT&T**; **77 kg** (171 lb); a sphere 88 cm across (NASA: 34.5 in, "slightly larger than a beach ball"), about **3,600** solar cells. Orbit **952 × 5,933 km, 44.8°**, 2 h 37 min. AT&T made an advance payment to NASA of **$2,680,982** for the launch (16 Feb 1962). NASA: "the first privately financed satellite"; hours after launch it relayed the first live TV pictures to France, a US flag waving outside the **Andover, Maine** station; each pass over the Atlantic gave about **18 minutes**; the Andover horn antenna was seven stories high and weighed 340 tons. Worked normally until November 1962, when radiation from the **Starfish Prime** high-altitude nuclear test affected its command channel, which stopped on **23 November 1962** (136 days); revived 20 Dec, transmitter failed 21 Feb 1963. Still in orbit.[^telstar][^telstarnasa]
- Syncom: built by **Hughes** for **NASA**. Syncom 1 (14 Feb 1963) fell silent seconds after its apogee motor fired; Syncom 2 (26 Jul 1963) was the first geosynchronous satellite; Syncom 3 (**19 Aug 1964**, **Delta D** #25, Cape Canaveral) the first **geostationary** one, at 180° longitude over the Pacific. Cylinders 71 cm × 39 cm; **68 kg** fuelled, **39 kg** in orbit: its solid apogee motor (about 1,431 m/s) took it from the transfer orbit to a near-circular 35,670 × 35,908 km orbit; 3,840 silicon solar cells gave 29 W "the **99 percent** of the time the spacecraft was in sunlight". Carried live TV of the **1964 Tokyo Olympics** (10–24 Oct) to the US; turned off in 1969, still in orbit. Arthur C. Clarke proposed the 24-hour orbit in *Wireless World*, 1945.[^syncom][^syncomnssdc][^syncomnasa]
- Early Bird (Intelsat I), COMSAT's first satellite, a Hughes geosynchronous design, launched **6 April 1965** on the second and last Delta D: "Global satellite communications had begun."[^comsat]
- Game: Telstar 1 asks for **136 days** of Data and makes the Company pay the whole Thor-Delta ($2.7M); Syncom 3 asks for **66 days** (19 Aug to 24 Oct 1964).
- Photos: [NASA, "Telstar Opened Era of Global Satellite Television"](https://www.nasa.gov/history/telstar-opened-era-of-global-satellite-television/) (Thor/Delta 316 launching Telstar 1; also on Wikimedia Commons as PD-USGov-NASA, NASA LOC-62P-141); the Andover horn antenna (Wikimedia Commons "Relay 1 antenna USA.jpg", source NASA, PD-USGov-NASA); [NASA image article "The First Geosynchronous Satellite"](https://www.nasa.gov/image-article/first-geosynchronous-satellite/) (a Syncom firing its motor, credit NASA); NASA Image and Video Library [9139573](https://images.nasa.gov/details/9139573) (a Thor-Delta launching Intelsat III, 21 May 1969, NASA/MSFC). Resized copies in `public/history/`.
