# Minigames: the facts each game teaches

Every Minigame ends on one fact. These are the facts, the words the game uses, and where they come from. Checked 15 Nov 2026. Numbers inside the games that are not listed here are **game estimates** and are labelled so in the code.

## Explorer (young players)

### Pluto is a dwarf planet of rock and ice
- The IAU reclassified Pluto as a dwarf planet in 2006. NASA: "Pluto was reclassified as a dwarf planet in 2006 by the IAU because other objects might cross its orbit." [NASA Science: Pluto Facts](https://science.nasa.gov/dwarf-planets/pluto/facts/)
- What it is made of: Pluto "probably has a rocky core surrounded by a mantle of water ice"; methane and nitrogen frost coat the surface. Same page.
- It lives in the Kuiper Belt, "populated with thousands of miniature icy worlds". Same page.
- Kids' page: [NASA Space Place: All About Pluto](https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/all-about-pluto/en/). Rovers: [The Mars Rovers](https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/mars-rovers/en/). Planets: [All About the Planets](https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/planets/en/). All three opened 15 Nov 2026.

### Mars rovers drive around Mars to collect and study samples
- Perseverance "seeks signs of ancient life and collects samples of rock and regolith for possible Earth return." [NASA Science: Perseverance](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/mars-2020-perseverance/)
- Curiosity drills rocks and studies them in its own lab: the "Cumberland" sample "was analyzed by the Sample Analysis at Mars lab inside the belly of NASA's Curiosity rover." [NASA Science: Curiosity](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/msl-curiosity/)

### The planets' order from the Sun, and their sizes
- Order: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune.
- Diameters (km): Mercury 4,879; Venus 12,104; Earth 12,756; Mars 6,792; Jupiter 142,984; Saturn 120,536; Uranus 51,118; Neptune 49,528. [NASA NSSDC Planetary Fact Sheet](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/)
- Smallest to biggest: Mercury, Mars, Venus, Earth, Neptune, Uranus, Saturn, Jupiter.

## Mission Director (realistic)

### Keeping electronics in their temperature range
- "All spacecraft components have a range of allowable temperatures that must be maintained to meet both survival and operational requirements during all mission phases." [NASA Small Spacecraft Technology State of the Art, ch. 7 Thermal Control](https://www.nasa.gov/smallsat-institute/sst-soa/thermal-control/)
- Heaters "are usually controlled by a thermostat or temperature sensor and used in cold environments to maintain battery temperatures, typically for components with the narrowest temperature limits." Same chapter.
- Radiators shed heat by radiating it "effectively to deep space"; heat in = heat out + heat stored. Same chapter.
- Each component's survival and operating limits in the game are **game estimates**, chosen to look like typical small-satellite ranges (batteries narrowest).

### CO₂ emergency: Apollo 13's square canisters in round holes
- Apollo 13 launched 11 April 1970; the explosion came at 9:08 p.m. CST on 13 April; it landed on 17 April. [NASA: Apollo 13 Mission Details](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/apollo/apollo-13-mission-details/)
- "The LM was designed to support two men for two days and was being asked to care for three men for about four days." Same page.
- "After a day and a half in the LM, a warning light showed that the carbon dioxide had built up to a dangerous level." Same page.
- "The square canisters from the command module were not compatible with the round openings in the lunar module environmental system." Same page.
- "Mission control devised a way to attach the CM canisters to the LM system by using plastic bags, cardboard" and tape. Same page.
- Lithium hydroxide scrubs CO₂ from the spacecraft air; the crew installed the "mail box" on 14 April 1970. [NASA image: Putting a Square Peg in a Round Hole](https://www.nasa.gov/image-article/putting-square-peg-round-hole/), [NASA image: Apollo 13 crew installing "Mail Box"](https://www.nasa.gov/image-article/apollo-13-crew-installing-mail-box-purging-carbon-dioxide-from-lunar-module-april-14-1970/)
- CO₂ levels, rise rates and piece counts in the game are **game estimates**.

### Deep-space loss of signal: Voyager 2, 2023
- "A series of planned commands sent to NASA's Voyager 2 spacecraft July 21 inadvertently caused the antenna to point 2 degrees away from Earth." The Deep Space Network then picked up only a carrier signal, too faint to extract data. Canberra sent a command to turn the antenna back; with a one-way light time of 18.5 hours, it took 37 hours to learn whether it worked. Full communications were restored by 4 August 2023. Canberra's DSS43 is the only antenna that can send commands to Voyager 2. Voyager 2 was about 19.9 billion km from Earth. [NASA: Mission Update: Voyager 2 Communications Pause](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/voyager-program/voyager-2/nasa-mission-update-voyager-2-communications-pause/)
- The Deep Space Network's three complexes are near Goldstone (California), Madrid (Spain) and Canberra (Australia); their "strategic placement … permits constant communication with spacecraft as our planet rotates". [NASA: Deep Space Network](https://www.nasa.gov/communicating-with-missions/dsn/)
- Signal strength falls with the square of distance (the inverse-square law, "space loss"). [NASA Science: Basics of Space Flight, ch. 6 Electromagnetics](https://science.nasa.gov/learn/basics-of-space-flight/chapter6-1/). DSN dishes range from 26 to 70 m. [NASA: Antennas of the Deep Space Network](https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/somd/space-communications-navigation-program/antennas-of-the-dsn/)
- A spacecraft's receiver "locks onto the signal using an internal circuit called a phase-locked loop"; a pure tone is called a carrier. [NASA Science: Basics of Space Flight, ch. 10 Telecom](https://science.nasa.gov/learn/basics-of-space-flight/chapter10-1/)
- The game's power budget, dB numbers and station timings are **game estimates** built on these rules.
